LATENCY PERCEPTION TEST
two-interval forced choice · adaptive staircase · ~60 seconds

This measures the smallest audio delay you can actually detect while playing. Not your reaction time — that's around 200 ms and has nothing to do with this. Not your rhythmic accuracy either. Just the point where a delay between your hands and the sound stops being invisible to you.

It exists to settle one question: a guitar rig here measures 8.02 ms round trip, cable loopback, driven down from 29 ms over one night. Is going lower still worth anything, or is it already under the floor of human perception? You cannot answer that from the milliseconds alone. You have to measure the listener.

How it runs

  • Each trial gives you two takes. Tap space four times in each, steady and moderate — think eighth notes, two or three taps a second.
  • Every tap fires a click. In one of the two takes that click is delayed. Which take, and by how much, is randomised.
  • You say which one felt delayed — ← first, → second. You must pick. Guess when you can't tell; guessing is what the maths is built to handle.
  • The delay chases you: it shrinks after two right in a row, grows after one wrong. It converges on the size you catch about 71% of the time.

What to feel for

Not an echo. The tell is that the click feels detached from your finger — the note lands a hair after the key and the groove drags behind you. Tap firmly and evenly. Sloppy tapping hides small delays completely, and it will cost you a real result.

You're on a touch device, so tap the on-screen pad instead of the spacebar. That works — screen latency sits under both takes equally and cancels out, exactly as a keyboard's does. But a finger on glass is less steady than a finger on a key, and that extra jitter inflates the result. Read your number as an upper bound, and if the answer matters to you, run it again on a laptop with a real keyboard.

Headphones or monitors, not laptop speakers if you can help it. Bluetooth is fine here — its latency is constant across both takes, so it cancels. Close anything else making sound. This runs entirely in your browser; nothing is recorded or sent anywhere.

THIS PAGE'S OWN LATENCY
read this before you trust the number

Underneath every measurement here sits a pedestal: the fixed delay from keypress to sound that exists before this test adds anything at all. Your keyboard, your OS, this browser and your output device all put something into it.

Why that doesn't wreck the result. This is a difference measurement. Both takes in a trial ride on the identical pedestal; the only thing that differs between them is the delay I add, and that is scheduled on the audio hardware clock to a fraction of a millisecond. The pedestal cancels. It does not inflate the number the way it would in a "does this feel laggy to you?" test.

The honest caveat. Delay-detection thresholds do grow somewhat as the baseline delay grows. Measured on a pedestal of roughly ? ms, your result is best read as a mild overestimate of what you would detect at near-zero baseline — not an underestimate. I do not subtract the pedestal from the result, because subtracting a fixed offset from a difference threshold is arithmetically meaningless. A number that looks precise and means the wrong thing is worse than an honest range.

Unknown and unmeasurable from inside a browser: keyboard scan, USB/HID polling and OS input latency, typically 5–25 ms combined. No web API reports it. It is part of the pedestal, it is identical across both takes, and so it too cancels from the comparison.
