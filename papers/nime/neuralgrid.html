NeuralGrid: A Physical Interface for Live Sequencing and Improvisation Using Adaptive Neural Models

Emory Smith · Ajay Kapur · Jake Cheng

EMGOR / California Institute of the Arts

Draft — NIME target

Abstract

NeuralGrid is a sensor-dense physical controller whose models speak a symbolic vocabulary before they speak sound. Pads, ribbons, lidar, and contact microphones are smoothed and interpolated into a coherent output format defined by the dimensional space the sensors represent. A first model interprets gesture through a symbolic vocabulary borrowed from tabla — where bols are spoken strokes, theka holds a cycle, and tihai and kaida act as geometric transforms over stroke sequences — so phrases can be played, combined, varied, and spoken aloud. A second model translates that symbolic stream onto any synthesis engine through seven perceptual axes. The translator is deaf by design: it never hears what it renders, which keeps model latency structurally outside the audio path. Parameter mapping across OSC and MIDI lets the same stream drive complex interfaces and mechanical instruments, including the Machine Lab.

Keywords: grid controller, symbolic vocabulary, tabla bols, perceptual coordinates, Raspberry Pi, Machine Lab

1. Introduction

Reflecting on the state of the Machine Lab system, a universal controller is the logical next step: something pre-mapped to the OSC addresses around the room, able to manage basic aspects of the server itself. Turning NeuralGrid on launches the server, and anyone new to the room can immediately and intuitively play every instrument in it — the room as an instrument rather than a composition platform.

The instrument built toward this separates the problem in two: a model that decides what is being played, expressed symbolically, and a model that decides what that should sound like, expressed as coordinates in a perceptual space. Neither one sits in the real-time path.

2. Two Layers, and the First One Is Language

2.1 The symbolic layer

The design turns on a borrowing from tabla. Bols are the spoken vocabulary of strokes — a phrase can be said as readily as played. Theka and taal hold a cycle, and tihai and kaida operate as geometric transforms over sequences of bols. That is a complete, tested grammar for playing, combining, and varying phrases, and it is symbolic and speakable by construction — which is precisely what generative pattern variation keeps trying to reinvent.

The first model therefore interprets gesture from spatial input through a symbolic vocabulary of this kind: recognizing phrases, holding a consistent cycle, and applying generative modulation as transforms over the symbol sequence rather than over audio. The output is vocally synthesizable, and the vocabulary has room to extend into English symbolism.

This is a borrowing from a living tradition and the paper treats it as one, named and cited to the tabla scholarship rather than presented as a found abstraction.

2.2 The translator layer

The second model maps symbolic output onto any synthesis engine through seven perceptual axes: frequency, loudness, harmonicity, rate (temporal modulation), scale (spectral modulation), space, and time. Hardcoded regions of the translator's behavior define specific instruments; loosening them lets more of the instrument be generatively shaped, until the whole thing is authored by the translator's reactions to the player.

The translator is deaf. It never hears what it renders — it responds to the player and to the symbol stream, not to the resulting sound. That separation is the design: a model that never listens never has to keep up, so model latency stays structurally outside the audio path.

2.3 Hardware

A grid of RGB pads with force sensing beneath them, ribbon sensors along the edges, lidar at the corners, and contact mics inside the enclosure as additional sensors, all running to a Raspberry Pi. Sensor input is smoothed and interpolated into a coherent output format defined by the dimensional space the sensors represent, rather than by what each individual sensor happens to emit.

2.4 Why the split matters

The model never sits in the hot path. A real-time layer reads sensors, plays notes, and drives LEDs without blocking on anything, while the models run beside it and stage their results for an atomic swap on a boundary. If a model takes 200 ms, nothing is late.

3. Current Status

Mid-build. The first encoder is working on hardware, with sensor smoothing and interpolation producing the coherent output format described above. The symbolic vocabulary and the translator are the next two milestones.

4. Future Work

Extend from the working encoder to the full sensor complement; implement the symbolic vocabulary and its transforms; then the translator and the seven-axis mapping. The Machine Lab renderer and a browser-based controller/renderer pair are the two deployment targets that would let a reader try the system without building anything. Target venue: NIME.
