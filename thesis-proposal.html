01 · WORKING TITLE

Digital Lutherie and Expression for the Electronic Artist: A 2027 Retrospective and Prospectus

the attackA “retrospective” is a topic. It names a territory and decides nothing. What does this title commit you to?

It commits me to two things. Retrospective: I look back at the decade in which the tools of digital lutherie — single-board computers, microcontrollers, audio languages, fabrication, and now AI — matured far enough that one person can build a complete instrument as a single act. Prospectus: I say where that goes, for expression, for the electronic artist, and for the economies that surround both.

The retrospective is what I have already built. The prospectus is what I am arguing. The title is deliberately not a question, because the question lives underneath it — next slide.

02 · PRELIMINARY RESEARCH QUESTION

What changes when the luthier and the performer are the same person?

the attackA question should change what you build, test, document, or notice. Which decision does yours force?
How does holding the whole instrument stack — operating system, hardware, synthesis, enclosure — in one practitioner’s hands change what an electronic artist can express, compared with the laptop-and-gear ecosystem it replaces, within the constraint of no institutional resources?

It forces me to build everything, not most things. If any layer is bought instead of made, the comparison collapses. That is the decision.

The evidence sub-question sits beneath it: when the OS, the instrument, and the method are built as one iterative act, what changes in the instrument’s expressive identity — and can that change be documented as a workflow other people can repeat?

The structure follows a third framing that isn’t a question at all: a threshold has been crossed in what one practitioner can build alone. What did it cost the instruments? What did it give them? Where does the practice go from here? Retrospective, retrospective, prospectus.

03 · TERRITORY & TENSION

Digital lutherie, and the thing that feels unresolved in it

the attackNIME has been here for twenty-five years. What is actually unresolved?

The territory is digital lutherie as an expressive practice for the electronic artist — not instrument design as an engineering discipline, and not music technology as a product category.

The tension: ten years ago a self-contained digital instrument demanded either institutional resources or a compromise. Sensor-rich controllers leaned on laptops; self-contained devices gave up compute or interface density. That gap has collapsed, and the field hasn’t fully noticed. The design literature — Fels on intimacy, Hunt and Wanderley on mapping, Jordà on identity, Cook’s “some instruments are meant to be stupid” — was written for a world where adding a sensor cost something. Now it costs nothing, and restraint becomes the whole discipline.

What’s unresolved is what that does to expression. Everyone can see the tools got cheaper. Almost nobody is documenting, from inside the practice, what it feels like to perform on a stack you built from the OS up — and what the instruments become when nothing about them was chosen by someone else.

04 · ARTISTIC OUTCOME OR EXPERIENCE

A performance where nothing onstage was bought that could have been built

the attack“Everyone benefits” is a mood, not an outcome. What does a person in the room actually get?

The ambition, said plainly: when luthiers can build faster, performers get instruments that fit them, audiences get performances that respond to the room, and the gaps between all three keep shrinking. Anyone can make anything. That is the claim I want to be true.

The artifact that has to prove it: a laptop-free performance at ICMC 2027 on the NEPTR performance system — a Raspberry Pi running Csound on my own operating system, a Teensy control surface, hyper-guitar and hyper-trumpet, a hemispherical speaker I printed, a second Pi rendering feedback visuals over a cable protocol I wrote, and NeuralGrid on the table. Every element self-built. Audience-reactive where it can be.

The written thesis explains it. The performance is it. If I had to cut one, I would cut the explanation.

05 · TECHNOLOGIES, TOOLS, METHODS

The stack I already have, and the one method that runs through all of it

the attackA list of tools isn’t a technical direction. Which of these is the research, and which is just the workshop?

The stack — already running, not proposed: DemiurgeOS (a flashable audio-first OS for the Pi 5, PipeWire underneath, a Rust supervisor that hot-reloads a patch graph); Csound for the instrument’s 63 effect modules; Teensy for control surfaces and audio I/O; 3D printing for every enclosure; OMNIPLEX, a Rust transport that carries audio, video, and control over one cable; and the NAMProcess opcode I wrote to put neural amp modeling inside Csound.

The method that is the research: building each layer with the layer below, iteratively, as one act — and using AI as a working collaborator at every step of it. I don’t give AI its own chapter. It shows up in every chapter, the way it shows up in every day of the work, because the compression of time between hitting a problem and having a solution is part of what collapsed the workflow in the first place.

The reflective method, planned: my git history across every project is a timeline of where my attention was and when it moved. I want to map that — later, deliberately, once every repo is under version control. Not this week.

06 · INTENDED AUDIENCE

Anyone curious enough to build their own

the attack“Anyone” is nobody. One system, one audience, one learning goal. Who?

Primary: the electronic artist who wants to make their own instruments and doesn’t know that they already can — the person I was three years ago, fighting a Pi in a class. This thesis is the document I needed then: not a tutorial, a map. Where the tools are, what they cost, what they cannot do, and what it feels like on the other side.

Secondary: the NIME / ICMC / Linux Audio research communities, who will read it as a case study — one whole stack, one practitioner, documented from inside — and as the writeup behind the individual papers.

And the reader who wants to track it: because the work is open and the progress is public, anyone can watch the direction shift over time. That is part of the offer.

07 · CONTRIBUTION

“My project contributes a practitioner’s map of the collapsed lutherie workflow, by building one whole stack and documenting it from inside.”

the attackPick one register. Which one carries the weight, and what happens to the others?

KNOWLEDGE · PRIMARY

A map of what the collapsed workflow costs, gives, and where it goes — written from inside the practice, not surveyed from above.

TECHNICAL

DemiurgeOS, the NEPTR system, NeuralGrid, the NAMProcess opcode. Real, running, open. They support the map; they are not the map.

ARTISTIC

A performance practice in which every element onstage is self-built. The proof the map points at.

EXPERIENTIAL

A changed relation between an electronic artist and their tools — the claim the other three make arguable.

One register carries the weight because the lecture is right: a thesis that contributes everything defends nothing. The others are not thrown away — they are what the map is drawn from.

And to be exact about what this thesis is not: it is not a longer version of Workflow of a Modern Digital Luthier. That paper is the methodology facet — one side of this. The thesis uses it, then goes where a journal article can’t: into the performance system, the OS, the controller, the economics, and forward.

09 · EVIDENCE & REFLECTION

What a reader can actually observe

the attack“I built it and it works” isn’t evidence. A four-axis reflection isn’t enough either. What would I see?
  • The prototypes themselves. Every system is open. Source, build files, configs, and enclosures are downloadable from this site. A reader can inspect the thing rather than take my word for it.
  • The performances. NIME, ICMC, and hopefully ICSC this year, plus recorded sets online. The claim is made in public, on a stage, where it can fail.
  • Their own results. If a reader builds it, they get the same instrument I have. Reproducibility is the evidence a practice-based claim can actually offer, and it is the one most theses can’t.
  • The data that exists. The latency perception test is real numeric work. Measured latencies, effect counts, costs, and build times are stated as numbers, not adjectives.
  • Reflection, as one lens among these — O’Modhrain’s playability / learnability / sonic range / robustness, per artifact, honest about where each one is weak.

The data is my own practice. The argument that it extends to other people is made in the landscape chapter, and it is argued, not measured. I’d rather say that plainly than pretend a survey I didn’t run.

10 · ONE DELIBERATE CONSTRAINT

Only the instruments I already have. Only my own practice as data.

the attackScope is an artistic constraint, not an apology. Can the claim survive if one feature disappears? What are you refusing to do?

I will not build a new instrument for this thesis. NEPTR, the hyper-instruments, the hemi speaker, We-Remote, NeuralGrid, the visual Pi — these exist or are already in flight. The thesis finishes and documents them. Nothing new gets started. (The hemi v2 and the visual Pi count as finishing.)

And the data is my own practice. This is a personal-practice paper. I am the performer, the luthier, and the subject, and I say so rather than borrow the authority of a study I haven’t run. Where the work faces other people — BOUBA’s users, other makers — I argue the connection; I don’t claim to have measured it. That keeps a whole evaluation regime, and the IRB that comes with it, out of a document that doesn’t need them.

Can the claim survive if one feature disappears? Yes — drop any satellite and NEPTR still performs. Drop NEPTR and the claim is gone. That is how I know which one is the core.

12 · NEXT STEP & THE OPEN QUESTION

What I’m doing next, and the question I can’t answer yet

the attackUseful feedback makes the next decision easier. What is the next decision?

Next step: this proposal, then a full draft of Chapter 1 — the methodology chapter — because it is the one the ROTURA paper already gives me most of, and because writing it will tell me whether the thesis is really a map or secretly a manual.

The most important unanswered question: what does “expression” measurably mean when the luthier and the performer are the same person? I can describe what it feels like. I cannot yet say what a reader would observe that is different from a performer on bought gear. If I can’t answer that by the end of Chapter 2, the title is wrong and I should know sooner rather than later.

What I most want to know from you: whether “expression” is the right word, or whether the thing I’m actually tracking has a better name.
